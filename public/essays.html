Resurrecting Frankenstein by Mary Shelley: A Case Study

How Frankenstein Came to Be

Frankenstein: The Modern Prometheus by Mary Wollstonecraft Shelley has permeated popular culture since its publication in 1818. Numerous adaptations of the story were produced, Halloween stores are selling costumes in the likeness of Frankenstein’s monster, and the novel itself is still in most bookstores today. Since Frankenstein’s publication, researchers have analyzed and written about her surviving drafts and manuscripts to provide more context as to how the novel was written, with Charles Robinson’s facsimile edition of Frankenstein as a valuable aid for this case study.[1] As a prominent 19th century female author, Mary Shelley solidified her legacy in the literary canon with Frankenstein.

Before she was known as the mother of horror, Mary Shelley was born Mary Godwin on August 30, 1797, to Mary Wollstonecraft, a feminist philosopher, and William Godwin, a writer and anarchist.[2] Both parents were notable philosophers and radical writers who wrote primarily about feminism and politics.[3] Wollstonecraft died 11 days after giving birth, so Mary Godwin spent most of her childhood attached to her emotionally distant yet intellectually encouraging father.[4] Her father encouraged her reading and informal education, and he regularly hosted friends with similar values: romantic writers like Samuel Taylor Coleridge and chemists like Humphry Davy.[5] Mary Godwin grew up interested in the sciences, reading books by Humphry Davy that discussed the “almost creative” power science gives humanity and attending lectures and experimental demonstrations of “gas, electricity, and phantasmagoria,” at seventeen years old.[6]

In 1814, Mary Godwin met Percy Bysshe Shelley during one of his many visits to William Godwin’s home.[7] Mary Godwin and Percy Shelley fell in love and developed a romantic affair during their regular visits to Wollstonecraft’s grave.[8] The couple eloped during one of their trips across Europe in 1814, and later moved into one of Percy Shelley’s apartments in London.[9] 

In February of 1815, Mary Godwin prematurely gave birth to her first child, who, according to her diary, was “not expected to live.”[10] Two weeks later, her newborn died without a name. The tragedy sat heavy on Mary Godwin’s heart and followed her into her dreams: that “[her] little baby came to life again - that it had only been cold & that [they] rubbed it by the fire & it lived,” only to wake up to the tragic reality of her loss.[11] The connection between birth and death (with the death of her mother after Mary Godwin was born and the death of Mary Godwin’s first child) and the image of reanimation was a repeated theme throughout her life and work.

The Legendary Trip to Geneva, Switzerland

In the summer of 1816, Mary Godwin, Percy Shelley, their six-month-old William Shelley, and Claire Clairmont traveled to Geneva, Switzerland to visit Shelley’s friends: the Romantic poet Lord Byron and his personal physician John William Polidori.[12] There, they discussed “the nature of the principle of life, and whether there was any probability of its ever being discovered and communicated,” conversations of which Mary Godwin was a “devout but nearly silent” listener to.[13]

When Geneva was struck by thunderstorms, the group was confined to Villa Diodati, where they passed the time by reading ghost stories. It was there that Lord Byron proposed a friendly ghost-story writing competition.[14] Mary Godwin struggled to come up with an idea until the night of June 16, 1816, when she had the dream that inspired the concept of Frankenstein:

 I saw – with shut eyes, but acute mental vision, – I saw the pale student of unhallowed arts kneeling beside the thing he had put together. I saw the hideous phantasm of a man stretched out, and then, on the working of some powerful engine, show signs of life, and stir with an uneasy, half vital motion. Frightful must it be; for supremely frightful would be the effect of any human endeavour to mock the stupendous mechanism of the Creator of the world. [...] He sleeps; but he is awakened; he opens his eyes; behold the horrid thing stands at his bedside, opening his curtains, and looking on him with yellow, watery, but speculative eyes.[15]

She started writing the next morning. The earliest version of her idea came to life as a transcript of her dream that Percy Shelley encouraged her to expand into a full-length novel.[16] Mary Godwin wanted to write a story that would terrify others the same way her nightmare terrified her.[17]

        Much of Mary Godwin’s travels were incorporated into the story of Frankenstein. During their stay at Villa Diodati, she used Percy Shelley’s descriptions of Lake Geneva and the surrounding mountainscapes from his diary entries as the setting of Victor Frankenstein’s boat trip with Elizabeth on their wedding night.[18] She travelled with Percy Shelley and Lord Byron while she wrote her novel, weaving into the text her experience visiting the Valley of Chamounix, providing the setting for Frankenstein’s travels with his father, brother, and Elizabeth.[19] After their return from Geneva, Percy Shelley received news of Harriet Shelley’s suicide and promptly married Mary Godwin, now Mary Wollstonecraft Shelley, fifteen days later.[20] Apparently unperturbed by the recent tragedy, Mary Shelley spent the rest of that summer writing her novel, until she finished the first draft in April of 1817.[21] 

Mary Shelley proved to be particular and detail-oriented when it came to revising her novel. As shown in her handwritten manuscripts, several of the letters’ dates were crossed out and rewritten to ensure the dates matched up with the corresponding year.[22] Her meticulous revisions revealed her intention with the timeline in which Frankenstein is set: with the first letter of the novel dated December 11, 1796, and the last letter dated about nine months later, September 12, 1797– thirteen days after Mary Shelley was born and two days after her mother died.[23] She planned the events of Frankenstein to transpire parallel to the duration of her mother’s fatal pregnancy.[24] These corrections make explicit her intent to express her personal experiences and feelings about birth, death, and grief in the context of horror.

Publishing

        Throughout the writing process, Percy Shelley was her editor. The two were known to have collaborated in the past, spending nights swapping manuscripts and reading each other’s work.[25] Based on Charles Robinson’s analysis of the original drafts, Percy Shelley likely read each chapter as she wrote it, acting as a “midwife” to bring her work to fruition,[26] with Mary Shelley leaving margins on the left of each page for his notes and suggestions.[27] From April to May of 1817, the couple worked on editing and transcribing the draft notebooks that were then fair-copied into three volumes.[28] By May 1817, the novel was ready to be published.[29]

        Percy Shelley handled all aspects of the publishing process. In the 19th century, it was up to authors to develop relationships with publishing houses and represent themselves. Percy Shelley reached out to John Murray, Lord Byron’s publisher, on May 26, 1817, with the Frankenstein manuscript, and while Murray enjoyed the read, he did not want to buy the copyright.[30] Percy Shelley then tried his own publisher, Charles Ollier, asking if he could publish the manuscript written by an anonymous “friend,” to no avail.[31] It wasn’t until September of 1817 that Lackington, Hughes, Harding, Mavor & Jones agreed to publish the novel, giving the author one-third of the profits made from sales.[32] 

News of the novel’s production was announced in the British Critic in October of 1817 and the publishing date was officially announced in the London Times on December 30, 1817.[33] Only a few days later, Frankenstein: or, the Modern Prometheus rang in the new year with its official publication in three volumes. The initial print run was 500 copies.[34] After gaining the attention of several literary critics, play adaptations of the tale like Richard Brinsley Peake’s Presumption gained traction.[35] The success of the play inspired William Godwin to edit and send a second edition of Frankenstein to be published in two volumes by G. and W. B. Whittaker in 1823.[36] The 1823 edition of Frankenstein named Mary Wollstonecraft Shelley as author of the work for the first time, disproving those who assumed the author was actually the late[37] Percy Shelley and shocking others with the audacity of her female authorship.[38] 

In 1831, Henry Colburn and Richard Bentley launched the Standard Novel Series, where they published books that were previously sold in two- or three-volume sets as single volume novels for a significantly lower price, making books more accessible and reaching larger audiences.[39] As part of the Standard Novel series, Frankenstein was published on October 31, 1831, in one volume in 4,020 copies, a significantly bigger print run than the first two editions.[40] This edition included an Introduction to the text written by Mary Shelley herself and a frontispiece engraving that was produced by William Chevalier from Theodor von Holst’s illustration depicting Victor Frankenstein’s disgust with his own creation.[41] In her Introduction, Mary Shelley told the story of how she came to write the novel, bringing to light the epic ghost-writing competition that has become legend over the years.

With the new 1831 edition of Frankenstein came some deviations from the original 1818 text that sparked some to claim the alterations make the 1831 edition a completely different novel. While Mary Shelley herself stated in her Introduction that the changes were small and mostly in style, others claim the changes were significant enough to alter the meaning of the story.[42] In one of Mary Shelley’s copies of the 1818 text, she noted that if there were to be another edition of the novel, she would rewrite the first two chapters completely as she felt they were “tame and ill arranged - the language sometimes childish,” which she did for the 1831 edition.[43] Some other notable changes to the text were to Elizabeth’s lineage, making her a close family friend of Victor’s rather than his blood cousin, removing any explicit incest from the narrative.[44] The 1831 edition also drew clearer parallels between Victor and Walkton to emphasize Victor’s negligence as a creator with newly written passages.[45] It wasn’t until a year after London’s Standard Novel Series edition of Frankenstein that the first American publisher, Carey, Lea & Blanchard in Philadelphia, published the novel in the United States.[46]

Reception

Despite Frankenstein’s initial small print-run in 1818, Frankenstein was reviewed by several critics and literary magazines expressing their admiration for the boldness of the novel. The major underlying compliment to the novel from many reviewers was on the originality of the story. Out of the eleven reviews retrieved for this case study, the only one that did not comment on the creativity of the novel was the Monthly Review, which had nothing complimentary to say about the novel at all.[47] The “original genius” and “power of expression” were sentiments reviewers frequently shared about the novel.[48] The writing style and the description of the landscapes were noted by many as the novel’s core strengths. With inspiration for these landscapes from Mary Shelley’s trips as she wrote the novel, her vision came to life, and the “fancy and language with which the work is written” resonated with critics who highlighted passages of Victor’s travels to Chamounix as testimony to her writing power.[49] A few reviewers appreciated the novel’s understanding of the “human mind” and “human heart,” whether it be through empathizing with Frankenstein’s creature or with Frankenstein’s misery and regret.[50] The Athenarum published Percy Shelley’s review posthumously in 1832, where he claims that the strength of the novel stems from its exhibition of “powerful and profound emotion,” and that its creativity and ability to move its readers is excellent.[51] 

Frankenstein also received criticism from unrelenting reviewers who faulted the writing quality and overall reading experience, the moral and theological basis of the tale, and the design of the creature itself. Even if some reviewers enjoyed the novel, many deduced the author’s inexperience, noting that the text bore “the marks of an unpracticed hand” and that the writing often felt rushed.[52] The “tedious writing” and the horrific nature of the tale led some reviewers to feel spiritually drained after reading the novel, comparing their reading experience to being “over-dosed with laudanum, or hag-ridden by the night-mare.”[53] Other critics were scandalized by the shocking nature of the tale, despite expressing their understanding of the author’s intent to tell a cautionary tale; reviewers at The Edinburgh Magazine and Literary Mischellany expressed discomfort with the idea of ascribing the term “Creator” to a mere human.[54] Though Percy Shelley’s preface in the 1818 edition seemed to soothe the troubled consciousnesses of the reviewers at The Belle Assemblée, they too were concerned about the impiety of the novel.[55] Others wrote more scathing criticism on Mary Shelley’s Frankenstein, claiming that the horrific tale was unwarranted in its fictional cruelty, that it was “too grotesque and bizarre” to be considered for literary merit, and that the story was lacking in morality completely.[56] Reviewers like The Quarterly Review questioned the mysterious author’s sanity and health for having written such a novel.[57] The disappointment in the morality of Mary Shelley’s Frankenstein cumulates with the critique of the form of Frankenstein’s creature itself. Some reviewers cited the sheer size and ugliness of the eight-foot-tall figure as the “most unskillful thing in the book,” questioning why Frankenstein didn’t opt to build a smaller creature.[58] The most amusing of such criticisms came from The Belle Assemblée, which suggested Frankenstein’s creature be “about the size of an egg or a walnut,” critiquing the unrealistic nature of the physical and moral consequences of Frankenstein’s creation.[59]

While many literary critics reviewed Frankenstein before the author’s true identity was revealed, magazines like The London Literary Gazette made sure to note the femininity of the writing after Mary Shelley claimed authorship of the novel, backhandedly claiming that “Frankenstein is certainly one of the most original works that ever proceeded from a female pen.”[60] Editors of later editions of Frankenstein debated Mary Shelley’s authorship in attempts to diminish her creative ownership of the novel and its subsequent success. Despite the tragedies in Mary Shelley’s life that can easily be assumed to be inspirations for her novel, like the death of her novel and her recurring nightmare of her dead baby coming back to life, many contemporary editors like James Rieger and Marie-Hélène Huct have pushed to give Percy Shelley co-author status for the creation of Frankenstein.[61] In the original manuscript drafts, Percy Shelley suggested that Frankenstein’s trip to England be his idea, rather than his father’s.[62] Rieger used this suggestion to assume that Percy Shelley had come up with the idea for this trip to create the female companion entirely, which was disproven by Robinson’s analysis of the manuscript, which found that the passages about the female companion were written well before Percy Shelley wrote this suggestion.[63]

Since then, Frankenstein’s legacy has grown from its birthplace in Geneva, Switzerland to its influence in the policies and culture of London, England, and across the seas as a horror icon in the United States. At least ten different plays inspired by the novel successfully performed in theaters in England from 1823-1849, the image of Frankenstein’s monster has been co-opted for political use, derogatorily depicting targeted groups of people as “revolting mobs” to disempower them, and hundreds of film and television adaptations have been released internationally since[64].

In your first paragraph, you make the claim that her work has been adapted and recreated a lot. Because of this claim's placement in the paragraph, I originally thought this was the thesis, but obviously that's not the focus of this paper. 

At the end of the introduction section, you mention Wollstonecraft Shelley's interest in birth & death. And this seems to be a somewhat repeated theme throughout your essay, but if you intend for this to be the thesis, I'd move it up to the first (or second if you have to) paragraph, integrate it more throughout, and come back to it at your conclusion. 

It seems like maybe the thesis of what's already here is that the events of Wollstonecraft Shelley's personal life and her own identity greatly impacted her work and its public reception. If this is the thesis you want to stick with, I'd write a thesis sentence that describes this, add it to the first (maybbeeeee 2nd) paragraph, and then restate it in the conclusion.
